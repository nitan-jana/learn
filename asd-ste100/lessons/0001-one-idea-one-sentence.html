Lesson 1 · ASD-STE100

One idea, one sentence

The whole standard rests on one habit: put a single idea in a sentence, and stop. Learn to do that by counting, and half of STE follows.

Sixty seconds of context

STE began in the late 1970s as AECMA Simplified English, first released in 1986, for aircraft maintenance manuals read by mechanics who did not speak English natively. About STE

ASD-STE100 is a controlled natural language: a deliberately restricted subset of English, defined so that a sentence cannot be read two ways. It has two parts — 53 writing rules in 9 sections, and a dictionary of about 900 approved words, each approved with exactly one meaning and one part of speech. (ASD)

It was built for maintenance procedures, where a misread instruction injures somebody. That origin is worth keeping in mind, because it explains the severity of the rules. But ASD is explicit that the benefits "are indeed applicable across all sectors", and that its principles "can significantly improve the readability and clarity of technical documentation across any industry." (FAQ)

You are going to learn it one rule at a time. Today: sentence length.

Today's rule

Rule 5.1 · Procedural writing

“Write short sentences. Use a maximum of 20 words in each sentence.”

Rule 5.2 · Procedural writing

“Write only one instruction in each sentence unless two or more actions occur at the same time.”

Rule 6.3 · Descriptive writing

“Write short sentences. Use a maximum of 25 words in each sentence.”

Rule 4.2 · Sentences

“Do not omit words or use contractions to make your sentences shorter.”

All four rules are quoted verbatim from ASD-STE100 Issue 9, Part 1, pages 1‑5‑1, 1‑6‑4, and 1‑4‑1. One nuance: a note inside a procedure is exempt from rule 5.1 and gets the descriptive limit of 25 words, because a note carries information and not an instruction.

Four rules, one idea. Three things are happening in it, and only the first is arithmetic.

  1. A ceiling. Twenty words is not a target. It is the point past which a reader has to hold too much in working memory to parse the sentence in one pass.
  2. A unit. One sentence carries one instruction. The reader's eye leaves the page to do the work, and must come back to a clean boundary.
  3. A floor. Rule 4.2 forbids buying the word count by deleting words or contracting them. Telegraphic English — Remove panel, disconnect cable — is a violation, not a shortcut. And rule 4.5 then requires the article you were tempted to drop.

That third clause is the one people miss. The limits are not a compression target; they are a signal that the sentence is carrying more than one idea. The fix is to split, not to squeeze.

What the split looks like

Before · 34 words, 1 sentence Once the container has finished building, which can take a couple of minutes on a cold cache, you can run the seed script, although you should check that the database is actually reachable first.
After · 3 sentences, longest is 11 words Wait for the container to build. A cold cache can make this take two minutes. Make sure that the database is reachable, then run the seed script.

Here is the standard's own worked example. It is the one non-aerospace example in the sentence-length rule, and it splits on exactly the same seam: the list of things a phone does was a second sentence hiding inside the first.

Non-STE · 31 words A smartphone is a cellular telephone that has an integrated computer and many other functions, such as an operating system, internet browsing as well as the ability to run software applications.
STE · 15 words, then 16 A smartphone is a cellular telephone that has an integrated computer and many other functions. It includes an operating system and an internet browser, and it can also operate software applications.

Notice what the split did beyond shortening. It put the check before the action, where the reader needs it. The original buried it in a trailing clause, after the instruction it was meant to guard. Long sentences hide ordering errors; splitting exposes them.

For your own writing

Your mission is blog posts, product copy, and posts for engineers — not maintenance manuals. So be deliberate about what you take.

Take the ceiling as a diagnostic. When a sentence in your draft runs past ~25 words, it is usually carrying two ideas, and one of them is being smuggled in a subordinate clause. Split it, read both halves, and keep the better one. That is true of prose that will never see an STE checker.

Take the floor absolutely. Never buy brevity by dropping words. Your existing voice already agrees with this.

Leave the uniformity. A maintenance manual wants every sentence the same shape; your posts do not. Varying rhythm — a 20-word sentence, then a four-word one — is how your prose lands. STE forbids the long one; you should not. Applying full STE compliance to a LinkedIn post would produce something correct and dead.
