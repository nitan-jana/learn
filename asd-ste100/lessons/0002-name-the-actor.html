Name the actor

A passive sentence can be perfectly clear and still be missing the one thing the reader needs: who does this. Learn the test, then the four fixes.

Today's rules

Rule 3.6 · Verbs

“Use the active voice. In descriptive writing, you can use the passive voice only if the agent is unknown.”

Rule 3.7 · Verbs

“Use an approved verb to describe an action, not a noun or other parts of speech.”

Quoted from Issue 9, pages 1‑3‑5 and 1‑3‑9. The standard defines the agent as “the person or thing that does the action.”

These two rules are one move. Rule 3.6 puts the actor in the subject. Rule 3.7 puts the action in the verb. Together they answer the two questions a reader is always asking: who, and does what.

The test

The standard gives a test sharp enough to use at speed. Look at the verb and ask “by whom, or by what?” If the sentence answers, it is passive.

Passive — answers the question The circuits are connected by a switching relay.
Active A switching relay connects the circuits.

The word by makes those easy. The dangerous passives are the ones with no by at all: The dimensions are given in the table. The main gear leg is held in its position. Ask “by what?” and the sentence has no answer — which means the writer knew something the reader now does not. Held by what? That is usually the sentence worth rewriting.

One thing that looks passive and is not. A past participle used as an adjective: Examine all parts of the disassembled unit. When the unit is fully disassembled, clean the parts. Rule 3.3 allows this before a noun, or after to be, to become and to stay, because it describes a condition, not an action. The checker built into the drills below cannot tell the difference and will flag them. Neither can most commercial checkers. You have to.

The four fixes

The standard gives four methods. The full table with examples is in the reference sheet — Passive to active, print it — but in short:

  1. Promote the agent that is already there after by.
  2. Use the real verb hiding in the sentence. Are used by the computer to calculate → the computer calculates.
  3. Command the reader, if this is a procedure. Oil and grease are to be removed → Remove oil and grease.
  4. Say you or we when no agent is given. You for the reader, we for your organisation.

The exception is the interesting part. Rule 3.6 permits the passive in descriptive text when the agent is genuinely unknown. The standard's example: During transmission, the data was corrupted. Force that into the active and you get Transmission corrupted the data — which names an agent that did not do it, and is now simply false. An honest passive beats a false active. This is the rule quietly admitting it is a heuristic for clarity, not a law of grammar.

For your own writing

Take rule 3.7 wholesale. Buried actions are the single biggest difference between writing that sounds like a competent engineer and writing that sounds like a press release. We did an evaluation of the approach versus we evaluated the approach. Your voice profile already pushes for specifics; 3.7 is the grammatical form of that instinct.

Take the agent test as a bug-finder. In your build posts, an agentless passive usually marks the exact spot where you know something the reader does not. The connection was closed — by what? Answering that in the draft is often the whole insight the post is about.

Ignore the ban on we-less description. STE offers you and we as method 4 for procedures written to a stranger. Your posts are first person by design. That is not a violation of anything; it is a different genre.
